Target: 0:40 · Why this subject & our route

We normally assume that one person has one unified self. But is it something we have proved, or simply how we usually experience ourselves?

What interests me most is that this phenomenon isn't entirely isolated. Throughout history, people have reported communicating with gods, spirits, deceased loved ones, or other seemingly independent creatures.

While the psychological mechanisms remain unclear, we can establish that repeated prayer, imagination, inner dialogue and specifically focused attention may play a role in how these experiences develop.

First, I'll distinguish forms of self-talk and explain why inner dialogue is relevant to tulpamancy. Secondly, I'll trace the term's Tibetan origins and Western reinterpretation, introduce the online communities, and explain what a tulpa means today. After that, I'll cover how people report creating tulpas and experiences such as wonderlands and switching. Next come involuntary companions and religious parallels, followed by what science knows. And finally, we'll consider moral and philosophical points of view on this phenomenon and conclude.

Target: 0:40 · Self-talk & the bridge to tulpamancy

Psychologists distinguish three types of self-talk.

[REVEAL] Inner speech is silent self-talk: rehearsing a conversation, solving a problem, or planning your next move.

[REVEAL] Private speech is saying it aloud: “Where did I put my keys?”

[REVEAL] Dialogic inner speech is an internal exchange between perspectives, as if two people were discussing a question. It’s a form of inner speech, not a later stage.

That back-and-forth offers a possible starting point for tulpamancy. Not ordinary inner dialogue, but speech directed specifically at a consistent, stable imagined mental figure.

Target: 0:40 · From Tibet to Western thought-forms

[REVEAL] The word tulpa is connected to the Tibetan term sprul pa, often translated in relation to emanation or manifestation in a Buddhist context.

[REVEAL] In 1929, the traveller Alexandra David-Néel popularised stories about thought-created beings in her book Magic and Mystery in Tibet.

[REVEAL] However, historians Natasha Mikles and Joseph Laycock argue that the modern Western idea owes a great deal to Theosophy and Western occult ideas about “thought-forms”, rather than being a direct continuation of Tibetan Buddhist practice.

That Western reinterpretation is the background to the online communities that came later.

Target: 0:45 · What is a tulpa today?

A tulpa is usually described as a companion or identity experienced as having some degree of independence within a person’s mind. The person who develops or lives with one may be called the host or tulpamancer.

These experiences are often deliberately cultivated and welcomed, though not always consciously created. Some people report having an inner companion long before discovering the term “tulpa”.

The form and vividness of communication can vary: some describe auditory, visual or tactile experiences, while others describe only a felt presence—a feeling that the companion is there.

Practitioners also report spontaneous replies, distinct preferences, disagreement, or the companion making its presence felt without being consciously called.

Target: 0:55 · Reported creation process

So how do people describe creating a tulpa? This deliberate practice is often called “forcing”.

[REVEAL] First comes an identity: imagining a companion with a name, an appearance, or a personality.

[REVEAL] Then comes attention: repeatedly bringing that companion to mind and spending time engaging with it.

[REVEAL] Next comes dialogue: talking to the companion internally or visualising interactions. At first, this may feel like writing both sides of a conversation.

[REVEAL] Then, slowly, little by little, some practitioners say the replies begin to feel less scripted, less predictable, and more autonomous—as though the companion is answering for itself.

That is the reported experience, not proof of a separate consciousness, of course. There is no fixed timeline or guaranteed outcome, and no established evidence on how easily it can be reversed.

Target: 1:05 · What practitioners report

Does it really “work”? Before answering, we need to ask what would count as working. If we mean feeling a presence or hearing an unexpected inner reply, practitioners do indeed report such experiences. But if we mean creating a separate conscious mind, that is an entirely different claim.

And what does “real” even mean? An experience can feel real to the person having it without being recognised as real by the outside world. Think of a dream: the experience itself is real, but that does not mean the world within it exists independently. This comparison does not explain tulpamancy, but it helps illustrate why subjective experience and objective reality are two different questions. After all, we cannot directly comprehend reality itself; we can only perceive and interpret it through our senses. Some experiences are more closely grounded in the external, material world, while others are primarily subjective.

[REVEAL]So how do people within the community describe their experiences?

A “wonderland” is an imagined shared environment, sometimes described as highly vivid.

“Switching” refers to a perceived change in who takes the lead.

Reports of body control describe actions that feel as though they are performed by the tulpa.

People also describe differences in identity, including preferences, memories, or skills.

These descriptions can overlap, and their meanings vary across the community.

Target: 0:55 · Religious practice & cultivated presence

Could religious practice cultivate something resembling a tulpa experience?

[REVEAL]In tulpa “forcing,” someone repeatedly imagines a companion and addresses them in inner dialogue. Over time, practitioners may describe replies that feel spontaneous, as though ANOTHER person is answering.

[REVEAL]Now consider some forms of prayer. A believer repeatedly addresses God as a personal presence, imagines being with God, and hopes for a response. The intention is a religious relationship, but parts of the practice may look familiar.

[REVEAL]The possible link is attention, imagination, and dialogue. Luhrmann and Morgain’s study of imaginative prayer found that some intense religious practice might shape a sharper imagery and more vivid spiritual experiences than usual prayer.

[REVEAL]So religious practices might cultivate a tulpa-like sense of presence, even without any intention of creating a tulpa. This doesn't necessarily mean the experiences are identical, but they may involve similar underlying neural mechanisms.

Target: 1:00 · Scientific evidence & open questions

What can research actually establish? There are three different questions.

[REVEAL] First: what do people report? Interviews and surveys document experiences of companionship, apparent autonomy, and changes in wellbeing.

[REVEAL] Take one example: in Isler’s 2017 survey, 78 percent of the 59 respondents to the mental-health question reported an overall positive impact.

[REVEAL] Second: what might explain the experiences? Related psychological research studies inner speech, imagination, attention, and absorption—becoming deeply immersed in an experience. The prayer research we just discussed gives us possible processes to investigate, rather than a complete explanation of tulpamancy.

[REVEAL] Third: does any of this establish a separate consciousness? These studies do not. Studying an experience and proving an independently conscious agent are different tasks.

[REVEAL] For a subject like this, an important step would be systematically documenting personal experiences. Also long term benefits and unwanted effects. Another approach could be measuring brain activity during "forcing" with fMRI(Functional Magnetic Resonance Imaging or EEG(Electroencephalography).

This makes tulpamancy an interesting field for research across psychology, sociology, neuroscience, and philosophy of mind.

Target: 0:45 · Risks, boundaries & consent

And this is not necessarily a harmless game with a guaranteed undo button.

[REVEAL] Internal habits can affect how people experience their thoughts, perceive the outer world, and even the sense themselves. And unfortunately, we lack strong longitudinal research.

[REVEAL] Some practitioners report companionship and mental-health benefits; others discuss unwanted experiences.

[REVEAL] The available surveys cannot establish how common either outcome is, or whether tulpamancy caused it.

[REVEAL] Having an unusual inner experience does not automatically mean someone has a mental disorder. But there are documented cases how such experience can trigger destabilization in already distressed individuals

[REVEAL] There is also a moral question: if someone regards their tulpa as a person with its own wishes, would abruptly stopping efforts to maintain this autonomous agent be considered abandoning someone or simply ending a mental practice? That brings us to philosophy.

Target: 0:50 · What makes a mind independent?

Suppose a tulpa seems to answer spontaneously. That suggests experienced agency: it feels as though the response comes from another source.

[REVEAL] Suppose it also has a stable personality, preferences and memories. That suggests a distinct identity within the person's mind.

[REVEAL] But neither of these automatically establishes subjective consciousness. As Consciousness means that there is something it feels like to be that subject, an inner point of view. And how could we ever verify that in a tulpa? We cannot directly inspect another person's consciousness either; we can only infer it from behaviour and other evidence.

Target: 0:40 · Conclusion: experience and evidence

To conclude, we need to distinguish experience from evidence. Reported companions raise questions about how we experience a self.

People describe: companionship and replies that feel autonomous; distinct preferences and identities; and, in reports of switching or body control, a change in who seems to be acting.

These reports give us something to study, but they do not settle these questions: how these experiences arise, whether they might lead to another consciousness, and what happens over time—including whether the experience can easily be reversed.
