Target: 0:35 · Introduction

We all talk to ourselves, sometimes even out loud. But has that inner voice ever seemed independent of you?

Imagine starting a conversation in your mind and receiving a response that feels as vivid and spontaneous as talking to a friend.

And to be clear, I'm not talking about a mental disorder. I'm talking about people who deliberately, or sometimes not so much, cultivate such experiences.

Would you call that imagination? A second personality? Or perhaps another mind? Today, I'd like to explore this phenomenon, known in the modern world as tulpamancy, and what it might tell us about consciousness and our sense of self.

Target: 0:40 · Why this subject & our route

We normally assume that one person has one unified self. But what does that assumption actually mean? Is it something we have proved, or simply how we usually experience ourselves?

What interests me most is that this phenomenon isn't entirely isolated. Throughout history, people have reported communicating with gods, spirits, deceased loved ones, or other seemingly independent beings. In some cases, repeated prayer, imagination, or inner dialogue may play a role in how these experiences develop. And yet, despite how fascinating these experiences are, we still don't fully understand the psychological mechanisms behind them.

I don't want to ridicule these experiences or accept every interpretation without evidence, but understand what they might reveal about the human mind.

First, I'll trace the term's Tibetan origins and Western reinterpretation, explain what a tulpa means today, and introduce the online communities. Then I'll cover how people report creating tulpas and experiences such as wonderlands and switching. Next come involuntary companions and religious parallels, followed by what science knows. Finally, we'll consider risks and consent, ask what makes a mind independent, and return to the mystery of the self to conclude.

Target: 0:40 · From Tibet to Western thought-forms

The word tulpa is connected to the Tibetan term sprul pa, often translated in relation to emanation or manifestation in a Buddhist context.

In 1929, the traveller Alexandra David-Néel popularised stories about thought-created beings in her book Magic and Mystery in Tibet. However, historians Natasha Mikles and Joseph Laycock argue that the modern Western idea owes a great deal to Theosophy and Western occult ideas about “thought-forms”, rather than being a direct continuation of Tibetan Buddhist practice.

Target: 0:45 · What is a tulpa today?

In modern online communities, a tulpa is usually described as a companion or identity experienced as having some degree of independence within a person's mind. The person who develops or lives with the tulpa may be called the host or tulpamancer.

The crucial word here is experienced. Practitioners often say the tulpa can respond without consciously scripting every word, express preferences, or disagree with them.

This is different from simply inventing a fictional character because practitioners describe the companion as subjectively autonomous.

Target: 0:35 · From esoteric idea to online community

The idea later circulated in Western paranormal and popular culture. In the early 2010s, online communities began to develop a much more practical and psychological interpretation: not necessarily a supernatural entity, but a companion cultivated through attention, imagination and repeated interaction.

People shared guides, described their progress, debated whether tulpas were independent, and created vocabulary for unusual experiences. This is important because a community does not merely describe an experience. It can also teach people what to notice, what to expect, and how to interpret what happens to them.

Anthropologist Samuel Veissière studied these communities through online ethnographic work. His research helps us understand tulpamancy as both an individual experience and a social phenomenon.

Target: 0:55 · Reported creation process

So how is it supposed to work? Online accounts describe a process often called forcing. Typically, a practitioner begins by imagining a companion, perhaps with a name, appearance or personality. Then they spend time directing attention toward it, holding internal conversations, or visualising interactions.

At first, the responses may feel completely intentional, like writing both sides of a dialogue. Over time, some practitioners report that the answers become less predictable and begin to feel as though they come from someone else.

There is no scientifically validated schedule for this. Some people report progress relatively quickly, while others describe months or much longer, and not everyone reports the same results. We also do not know whether the reported transition reflects one single mechanism.

This is a description of community practice, not a scientifically tested instruction manual. And it matters that deliberate changes to one's habitual inner experience may not feel easy to reverse.

Target: 1:05 · What practitioners report

Now for the more surprising part: does it actually work? That depends on what we mean by work. Do people report vivid, unusual experiences? Yes—ethnographic research documents them. Has science proved a second, independently conscious person exists? No. But how could we verify another subjective experience without visiting that person's mind?

Some describe a wonderland, an imagined environment shared with their tulpa, or switching, where the tulpa seems to take control of the body.

Others report different tastes, memories, or abilities. A tulpa might supposedly learn a skill while switched in that the host later struggles to perform. Does this reflect a difference in ability or how the person performs? Self-reports alone cannot tell us.

During switching, some practitioners describe retreating into a wonderland as vivid as the real world. Because of this, some worry that stopping forcing could leave their tulpa abandoned in that inner world, perhaps like being left in a dark room. This is a community interpretation, not an established fact, but it raises an ethical question: if someone regards a tulpa as a person, is ending the relationship simply stopping a mental habit, or something morally significant?

Target: 0:55 · Involuntary companions & religious parallels

Not every experience of an apparently independent inner presence begins with a deliberate decision to create a tulpa. People can describe spontaneous or uninvited companions, and community discussions sometimes interpret longstanding imaginary characters or intensely imagined beings in this way. But we should be careful: thinking about a dog, a fictional character, or an imaginary friend does not automatically create a tulpa.

This brings us to religion. Some religious people describe feeling God's presence or receiving answers during prayer. Anthropologist Tanya Luhrmann has studied how certain forms of prayer train attention to inner experiences, and how these experiences can become more vivid and meaningful.

Could similar psychological processes contribute to both reports? Possibly. Focused attention, expectation, and interpretation may overlap. But that does not prove that religious experiences are tulpas, nor does it settle whether God exists. Scientific comparison concerns how experiences are formed and understood, not the final truth of every belief.

Target: 1:00 · Scientific evidence & open questions

What does science actually tell us? First, anthropology and sociology can document how people describe tulpas, how communities form, and how shared expectations influence interpretation. That is a meaningful finding, but it is not the same as independently confirming every claim.

Second, psychology studies inner speech, mental imagery, attention, and absorption. Absorption is the tendency to become deeply immersed in an experience or imagined situation. Research on prayer has shown that sustained imaginative practices can make internal experiences feel more vivid and significant.

Third, neuroscience studies the systems involved in self-representation, agency, and distinguishing self-generated from externally generated experiences. But there is not yet a well-established neurological test that can tell us a tulpa is an independently conscious individual.

The research specifically on tulpamancy remains limited, often using small or self-selected communities and self-report. That makes it useful for generating hypotheses, but weak for strong causal claims. We should distinguish “people experience this” from “we know exactly how and why it happens”.

Target: 0:45 · Risks, boundaries & consent

A final practical point before the philosophical question: this is not necessarily a harmless game with a guaranteed undo button. Repeated attention and internal habits can affect how a person experiences their thoughts, but we lack strong longitudinal research showing how often tulpa experiences persist, fade, or cause problems.

Some practitioners report companionship and benefits; others discuss unwanted experiences or difficulty changing the relationship. The available surveys cannot establish how common either outcome is, or whether tulpamancy caused it. Having an unusual inner experience does not automatically mean someone has a mental disorder. The relevant concerns are distress, loss of control, and interference with daily life.

And if practitioners believe a tulpa has its own wishes, the question of consent becomes complicated. That brings us directly to philosophy.

Target: 0:50 · What makes a mind independent?

This is the question that interests me most. Suppose a tulpa seems to answer spontaneously. That suggests experienced agency: it feels as though the response comes from another source. Suppose it also has a stable personality, preferences and memories. That suggests a distinct identity within the person's experience.

But neither of these automatically establishes subjective consciousness. Consciousness means that there is something it feels like to be that subject, an inner point of view. How could we ever verify that in a tulpa? We cannot directly inspect another person's consciousness either; we infer it from behaviour and other evidence.

And reported switching makes the puzzle sharper: if one body can be experienced as controlled by different identities at different times, does that mean there are multiple conscious subjects, or different organisations of one person's mental processes? The reports raise the question. They do not settle it.

Target: 0:40 · Conclusion: the mystery of the self

So, can one brain contain more than one mind? We don't have a scientifically established answer in the sense that tulpamancy proves multiple independent consciousnesses.

But I think dismissing these accounts simply because they sound strange would also be a mistake. The experience of having a self, an inner voice, and control over our thoughts feels obvious until we try to explain exactly how it works.

My conclusion is simple: we should take unusual subjective experiences seriously, while remaining critical of the explanations we attach to them. Perhaps the most interesting mystery is not whether someone can create a second self, but how our ordinary sense of being one self is created in the first place.

So I'll leave you with the question: What would actually convince you that another mind exists?
